Computer Ethics

Topic: Swarm Surveillance

Ethical Question: Is sacrifice of privacy and movement freedom justified to catch criminals more efficiently?

What is "Swarm Surveillance?"

Swarm surveillance (interchangably used with "mass surveillance" in this article), refers to the use of a "swarm" of or many connected devices in a network to monitor, track, and/or map an area. For this article, we will refer frequently to one of the most relevant applications and representatives of contemporary swarm surveillance technology across North America today: Flock Cameras.

How to Maximize the Benefits of Swarm Surveillance Implementation

Flock cameras are a great example of an application of swarm surveillance, used to enforce the law and increase the protection of public spaces. The system supports over 1 million police investigations yearly by turning vehicle descriptions and movement into searchable data. These cameras can also notify authorities when flagged vehicles come within certain areas, and can also decrease the amount of crime occurring when these cameras are present in public spaces. Missing people can easily be discovered compared to the absence of this technology. Data collected from these cameras can also be used to monitor traffic flow, manage event perimeters, and better coordinate emergency response during evacuations or natural disasters.

Although many helpful benefits come from the use of swarm surveillance, there are many risks related to public safety and privacy, system security, and civil liberties. A method that can help maximize the benefits of surveillance while minimizing the risks that come with these systems is to use said cameras and data only when there is a clear purpose, such as parking lots, public areas, or the entrances of schools and work buildings. Notifying the people that they are being monitored through signs near the camera can also be helpful in these areas. This will allow only necessary information to be collected rather than collecting all unnecessary identification or tracking information. Another strategy that can be used is to use strong passwords, access controls, and encryption, and only allow authorized people such as school staff and admin to access surveillance data. Because Flock has stated that its data is shared with thousands of companies, it shows a risk of a data breach and misuse of data. Stolen surveillance footage or personal information can be used for harmful purposes. Lastly, swarm surveillance companies can be transparent and accountable by explaining clearly what cameras collect, why the data is collected, how long it is kept, and who can access it. The misuse of data should also be reported to the public rather than ignored, which allows people to understand fully how these systems work and how decisions are made.

The Other Perspective - Understanding the Issue

Before delving into the ethical debate surrounding swarm surveillance, it is important to first acknowledge its potential benefits. For example, Flock Safety cameras use Automated License Plate Recognition (ALPR) technology to help law enforcement investigate crimes such as vehicle theft, hit-and-runs, and other incidents involving vehicles. These cameras can also alert authorities when a vehicle associated with a missing person or an ongoing investigation is detected. Beyond law enforcement, similar networked camera systems can help monitor traffic patterns, improve public safety in certain locations, and assist emergency responders. When implemented with appropriate safeguards, these technologies can provide valuable information that would otherwise be difficult or time-consuming to obtain. However, the question remains whether these benefits justify the widespread collection and storage of information about ordinary citizens.

However, for the perspective or side that is against mass/swarm surveillance, though the benefits are certainly aknowledged, the ethical concerns are also undeniable.
Living in the digital age, defined by rapid technological advances, one must understand that contemporary swarm surveillance is nothing like traditional surveillance. Rather than having people monitoring an array of live surveillance feed, modern swarm surveillance involves massive arrays of surveillance devices pooling data together into a network that can be used to track people.
Though many mass surveillance systems claim to be only ALPRs, most if not all are actually using various AI and computer vision technologies in their backend to identify, profile and track people. Today, many mass surveillance devices and systems are being installed everywhere, especially with the cases with Flock Cameras, which has been the hot of the news for being installed in many places down in the US and even some parts of Canada today.
It should be a very scary thought, that there is being a profile built in a digital database of you, your friends, family, and kids; you are being photographed and tracked 24/7 of who you are, who you're with, where you are, where you go, on what day, at what time and more—from the house, to the school, to the workplace, to the gym, to the restaurant, to the station, and across the country.
And all of this profiling data, especially in the case of Flock, is being actively sold to the government.

Moreover, personal privacy aside, the people who operate these systems are unethical and unfit to operate such technology responsibly in the first place.
As the golden rule goes, do to others what you would want be done to you; clearly, mass surveillance services, especially Flock, do not practice what they preach when it comes to privacy and safety. Because when you decide to point the camera the other way and watch the watchers, they have the same privacy concerns as we do.

This lead to one of the officers preparing to draw her gun when walking up to the journalist after pulling him over; Flock boy here was trying to get this man killed!

Furthermore on responsibility and ethical operation, there is also the massive concerns regarding abuse of the data. All mass surveillance systems, just like with Flock Cameras, don't just track criminals, but everybody. This has led to cases such as a police officer making 25,000 searches related to his ex-girlfriend and her family. Also, living in a digital age where data is so powerful and valuable, there are thus concerns regarding the selling of this data to data brokers and AI companies, pointing to further concerns on privacy, responsibility and abuse.

The Other Perspective - Solving the Issue

From this viewpoint, there are mountains of downsides regarding swarm surveillance.
Thus from this perspective, the most effective solution would be to prevent its widespread deployment and remove existing systems that enable indiscriminate tracking of the public. Rather than relying on a network of cameras that continuously collects information about everyone, governments and communities should prioritize targeted investigations, traditional policing methods, and surveillance that is limited to specific, legitimate purposes. For example, license plate recognition could be restricted to narrowly defined investigations, with strict requirements for authorization, short data-retention periods, and independent oversight. These measures would help preserve some of the public safety benefits of surveillance without allowing a permanent record of people's movements to accumulate. However, restrictions alone may not be sufficient, as existing systems can still be misused, and enforcement depends on the willingness of companies and governments to comply. A stronger approach would combine strict legal limits, public accountability, and, where necessary, the removal of systems that cannot adequately protect civil liberties. Although these solutions may make certain investigations more difficult or expensive, public safety should not come at the cost of continous indiscriminant each and every person's daily movements.

Strategies for Encouraging Ethical Computing Pratices

As members of society and global citizens, it is our collective role to ensure the freedom and safety of each other. Some ways for one to do their part with regards to computer ethics and mass surveillance include:

At the Workplace:

  • Employees/technicians should research an organization's data practices and avoid participating in projects that enable unethical or indiscriminate surveillance
  • Management teams & executives in companies should ensure the organization's operations remain ethical or push for change otherwise
  • Employees should report unethical data practices and advocate for transparent policies, independent audits, and protections should be put in place for whistleblowers

At School:

  • Students should learn about privacy, digital rights, and ethical implications & impacts of technologies such as mass surveillance
  • Schools and STEM clubs should arrange discussions about benefits, risks, and appropriate limitations of (surveillance) technologies
  • Schools should be encouraged to help students assess privacy risks, explain data-collection practices, and consider alternatives for ethical computing, especially regarding surveillance systems

At Home:

  • Everyone should review app permissions, limit unnecessary data collection, use strong passwords, and discuss digital privacy with family members
  • People should spread credible research and information on surveillance and computer ethics throughout the (social) media and community discussions
  • Citizens should contact local government representatives, and lobby/advocate for policies to require transparency, proportionality, and oversight of surveillance systems
