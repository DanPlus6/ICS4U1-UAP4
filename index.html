Computer Ethics

Topic: Swarm Surveillance

Ethical Question: Is sacrifice of privacy and movement freedom justified to catch criminals more efficiently?

What is "Swarm Surveillance?"

Contemporary swarm surveillance, refers to an extension of traditional mass surveillance. "Swarm surveillance" involves a mass of surveillance devices connected in a network to monitor, track, and/or map a large area. For this article, we will refer frequently to one of the most relevant and representative applications of contemporary swarm surveillance technology across North America today: Flock Cameras.

What are "Flock Cameras?"

"Flock Cameras" are cameras for a swarm surveillance system, developed by the service/company Flock Safety. Surrounding ethical debate, along with sudden mass installation across the US and parts of Canada have made it the hot of the news as of recently.

How to Maximize the Benefits of Swarm Surveillance Implementation

Flock cameras are a great example of an application of swarm surveillance, used to enforce the law and increase the protection of public spaces. The system supports over 1 million police investigations yearly by turning vehicle descriptions and movement into searchable data. These cameras can also notify authorities when flagged vehicles come within certain areas, and can also decrease the amount of crime occurring when these cameras are present in public spaces. Missing people can easily be discovered compared to the absence of this technology. Data collected from these cameras can also be used to monitor traffic flow, manage event perimeters, and better coordinate emergency response during evacuations or natural disasters.

Although many helpful benefits come from the use of swarm surveillance, there are many risks related to public safety and privacy, system security, and civil liberties. A method that can help maximize the benefits of surveillance while minimizing the risks that come with these systems is to use said cameras and data only when there is a clear purpose, such as parking lots, public areas, or the entrances of schools and work buildings. Notifying the people that they are being monitored through signs near the camera can also be helpful in these areas. This will allow only necessary information to be collected rather than collecting all unnecessary identification or tracking information. Another strategy that can be used is to use strong passwords, access controls, and encryption, and only allow authorized people such as school staff and admin to access surveillance data. Because Flock has stated that its data is shared with thousands of companies, it shows a risk of a data breach and misuse of data. Stolen surveillance footage or personal information can be used for harmful purposes. Lastly, swarm surveillance companies can be transparent and accountable by explaining clearly what cameras collect, why the data is collected, how long it is kept, and who can access it. The misuse of data should also be reported to the public rather than ignored, which allows people to understand fully how these systems work and how decisions are made.

What is Flock?

Flock Safety is an American safety tech company, which claimed to have deployed over 120,000 cameras for their nationwide surveillance network across 49 states, said by Flock themselves to have captured billions of just vehicle scans every month. They record the movements of millions of people whether they're suspected of crime or not, making millions of dollars from police departments searching through their data network.
This development led to widespread public backlash; critics argue that Flock discretly built a hyperscale government surveillance dragnet without public vote or federal oversight, since any police department on the network can search across state lines. Security researchers mapped 335,701 Flock cameras in the network, creating a "Flock Surveillance Map," which Flock is trying to take down.

Swarm surveillance, especially seen in the predominant Flock Cameras, do have their benefits, hence why it remains a debated ethical issue and definitive resolution is yet found. Contemporary swarm surveillance such as Flock are nothing like traditional surveillance, where someone or multiple people monitor an array of surveillance footage. Though mass surveillance services claim they only use Automatic License Plate Readers (ALPRs) (which are already a tracking and privacy concern), they actually pool surveillance data from into a network—hence swarm surveillance. They use various computer vision, facial recognition, AI and data analysis tools in their backend. This is then used to track and catalogue everyone's movement and location, building a digital profile of every person in a database. This may have some benefits, such as helping law enforcement to go back-in-time for investigation crimes such as vehicle theft, hit-and-runs, and track people who are missing or under investigation. As such, the police and the government love mass surveillance services like Flock, which is the only reason they're even able to remain implemented in when taking into account the undeniable issues regarding mass surveillance in the first place.

For the sake of the rest of this article, let us Canadians assume a representative example of swarm surveillance, Flock Cameras, were installed around you.
Just as an individual person, it should already be a very scary thought, that there is being a profile built in a digital database of you, your friends, family, and kids. You're are being photographed and tracked 24/7 of who you are, who you're with, where you are, where you go, on what day, at what time and more—from the house, to the school, to the workplace, to the gym, to the restaurant, to the station, and across the country at every location outside your home.
Immediate sentiments aside, we must question the ethics and responsibility of swarm surveillance services' operatives, operations and also abuse—not just possessing mountains of data in the first place, but also how it is used, and in whose hands it ends in. Living in a digital age of rapid technological advancement, of information and communication technologies, data grows more valuable and powerful by the day. If all this powerful and valuable data of not just you but your friends, colleagues, family members and children end up in the wrong hands, there be dragons- actually, there already are.

Let's first consider the ethics and responsibility of swarm surveillance services.
Without your expressed consent in any way, shape or form- in fact already understanding the major populace's dissent in the first place, swarm surveillance services like Flock continue to record us 24/7. Swarm surveillance systems like Flock don't just record criminals, but everyone, and has even incorrectly linked innocent individuals to crimes. They do not allow us to opt-out of their system, with services like Flock stating that citizens have no practical choice but to be tracked in their nationwide database. Flock's CCO even personally stated in an interview that one can opt-out of their concurrent surveillance "if [one] opt[s]-out of society." It's as if swarm surveillance services like Flock are flipping the script, to say that you're guilty until proven innocent.

As the golden rule goes, do to others what you would want be done to you; clearly, mass surveillance services, do not practice what they preach when it comes to privacy and safety. When you decide to point the camera the other way, and watch the watchers, they have the same privacy concerns as we do.
Anthony Sistilli, a Canadian software engineer and YouTuber, setup an ALPR this September to exclusively track police cars, using the same technology swarm surveillance cameras like Flock use to track ordinary citizens. Sistilli would then receive a visit from officers expressing their privacy concerns, ironically mirroring the concerns of ordinary citizens about swarm surveillance: “One of them [the officers] took the time to specifically mention how scary it would be for them, because people would be able to find out where they lived, when they got on their shift and what they were doing throughout the day, and that that type of powerful information could put them personally at risk for any bad apples that might want to track them down” In addition, Flock's company executives have explicitly expressed their own concerns regarding being recorded or photographed.
In another case where an American investigative journalist, Brendan Keefe, publicly filmed a technician upgrading a Flock Camera, the technician quickly packed up and sped away in his van while calling 911, claiming to feel threatened and stalked. Not only do Flock and their employees ironically have the same privacy concerns as us, but also carry out unethical conduct. The technician said in the call that he "[didn't] know if [the journalist] was armed." This lead to Keefe being pulled over and detained by three police cars, with the officer who spoke to Keefe approaching with her hand on the butt of her gun; in other words, Flock boy here was trying to get this man killed! The technician also only only provided his first name when prompted during the 911 call, because "[he] [didn't] want them to hear [his] full name on the 911 call when they get it." The technician thus displays understanding that Keefe is affliated with a group who has ability to aquire the details of his call. Such an advanced thought is not something most people feeling truly threatened or being stalked can conceive, and it's also not the first time a Flock employee has called 911 on content creators, reporters, and journalists, and also not the first time employees have suggested that said people possessed firearms. Together this hints that Flock may be training employees to use the government, law enforcement and/or emergency services to oust opposition, further highlighting Flock and swarm surveillance services/companies' ethical morbidity and ethical "flexibility" in their conduct.

With much regarding ethical and responsible conduct said, let's talk about abuse. Swarm surveillance systems, like with Flock Cameras, don't just track criminals, but everybody. Accumulating so much valuable and powerful data, terrible concerns arise should the data fall in the wrong hands, but it already has.
There have been cases such as a police officer making 25,000 searches related to his ex-girlfriend and her family. Or the time an officer having a crush and searching her through camera feed, then tracking her down and almost causing a crash on the highway just to pull her over and ask her out.

The Other Perspective - Solving the Issue

There are mountains of undeniable issues regarding swarm surveillance.
A just and ethical system should not sacrifice the privacy, liberties, and even safeties of the many for bringing to justice the few. Thus, all surveillance services, systems, and alike should be rigorously investigated. Unethical and overreaching systems such as Flock should be abolished and prevented from implementation in the future.
(Unethical) swarm surveillance systems like Flock have managed to remain implemented despite widespread public backlash due to their number one customer being the government. In some cases regarding Flock journalism and investigation, government agents such as law enforcement officers have even suggested that "people should think twice about what they put on the media." The government's overreach, if continued or worsened through continous or further implementation of swarm surveillance and even attempts at media censorship are terrible indicators of the state our world has come to. But it was never the people for the government, rather the government was made for the people; thus there ought also be changes in the government.
Thus to conclude, we should shine light on the impacts and ethics of computing by spreading awareness in (social) media, rigorously investigating surveillance and other tech systems, and advocate for change in laws or policies to demand transparency from companies and mitigate threats to privacy, safety and liberty in computing operations.

Strategies for Encouraging Ethical Computing Pratices

As members of society and global citizens, it is our collective role to ensure the freedom and safety of each other. Some ways for one to do their part with regards to computer ethics and mass surveillance include:

At the Workplace:

  • Employees/technicians should research an organization's data practices and avoid participating in projects that enable unethical or indiscriminate surveillance
  • Management teams & executives in companies should ensure the organization's operations remain ethical or push for change otherwise
  • Employees should report unethical data practices and advocate for transparent policies, independent audits, and protections should be put in place for whistleblowers

At School:

  • Students should learn about privacy, digital rights, and ethical implications & impacts of technologies such as mass surveillance
  • Schools and STEM clubs should arrange discussions about benefits, risks, and appropriate limitations of (surveillance) technologies
  • Schools should be encouraged to help students assess privacy risks, explain data-collection practices, and consider alternatives for ethical computing, especially regarding surveillance systems

At Home:

  • Everyone should review app permissions, limit unnecessary data collection, use strong passwords, and discuss digital privacy with family members
  • People should spread credible research and information on surveillance and computer ethics throughout the (social) media and community discussions
  • Citizens should contact local government representatives, and lobby/advocate for policies to require transparency, proportionality, and oversight of surveillance systems
